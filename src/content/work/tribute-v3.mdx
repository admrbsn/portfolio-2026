After nearly a decade of incremental improvements, Tribute had grown into an
emotionally meaningful product used by millions, but the architecture underneath
had not kept pace. V3 was a full re-architecture, not a visual refresh: a rethink
of how creators and participants experience the product, delivered in about three
months by a team of four working one-week sprint cycles.

## Where it broke down

Three problems held the platform back. Nearly every major surface, from
onboarding and publishing to invitations, editing and checkout, had been improved
in isolation, so the experience was inconsistent and expensive to maintain. Users
kept asking for more control over themes and personality that the product never
gave them. And creators and participants were effectively using two different
products, losing the shared context that made a tribute feel like one thing.

## Research and discovery

I ran an intensive teardown of competing products, mapping journeys from creation
through completion, with Partiful and Paperless Post as reference points. A Maze
feature-ranking exercise confirmed that people expected structural features to
stay unobtrusive while emotional expression came first: video messages led, and
theme and visual customization ranked higher than we expected.

An early false-door concept placed the product page itself as the creation
experience. Testing showed people were unsure what to do next, so we pivoted to a
modal-based start that preserved clarity while teasing the new experience.

## What changed

Onboarding was rebuilt to feel expressive rather than like a homemade form,
teasing customization early and building immediate confidence. Post-launch
onboarding conversion reached 54%, more than doubling the historical baseline of
roughly 15 to 25%.

Payment moved from the very end of the flow, where it caused late-stage
abandonment and 16% accidental checkouts, to just after the draft stage, aligning
it with the moment users actually felt committed while keeping the product free to
start. Research also surfaced how much people loved emojis, which became the
foundation for animated, occasion-based backgrounds: balloons, fireworks,
graduation caps, seasonal themes. Creators and participants now share one
continuous space, separated by permissions rather than by separate interfaces.

## Changing how the team builds

The bigger shift was in process. Instead of designing static files for handoff, I
made functioning code the deliverable, using Figma only for targeted layout
exploration and never as the source of truth. Combined with Lean UX and one-week
sprints, decisions that used to take weeks landed in single sessions once working
prototypes replaced mockups. AI tooling like Relume generated sitemaps and
wireframes from plain-language descriptions, keeping continuity from sitemap to
high fidelity.

CSAT rose 20% alongside those gains. Beyond the numbers, V3 left a modern,
scalable foundation that makes iteration safer and experimentation cheaper. The
lasting change was reframing the design system not as a Figma library but as a
living system rooted in AI-assisted prototyping, where the deliverable is working
code.
